Part A · Lessons 1–2

Ratios

Learn chapter →

Know it

  • A ratio compares two amounts. 3 cups of flour and 2 cups of sugar make the ratio 3 : 2, said “3 to 2.”
  • Order matters. “Sugar to flour” puts the sugar first: 2 : 3.
  • Ratios can often be split into equal groups. Each group says “for every …”: for every 3 cups of flour, there are 2 cups of sugar.
  • A ratio can compare a part to the whole, too: 2 cups of sugar out of 5 cups in all is 2 : 5.

Example

6 circles and 9 squares: the ratio of circles to squares is 6 : 9.

They make 3 equal groups of 2 circles and 3 squares, so for every 2 circles, there are 3 squares.

Watch out: 9 : 6 is squares to circles, the other order. And “3 more squares” is a difference, not a ratio.

Part B · Lessons 3–5

Equivalent ratios

Learn chapter →

Know it

  • A batch is one full recipe. More batches use more of everything, but it tastes the same.
  • Two paint mixes are the same color when their ratios are equivalent.
  • Ratios are equivalent when you multiply (or divide) both numbers by the same number: 2 : 3 = 4 : 6 = 10 : 15.
  • To check two ratios, find what one number was multiplied by, then see if the other number was multiplied by the same.

Example

Lemonade uses 2 scoops of mix for 5 cups of water. 3 batches:

3 × 2 = 6 scoops and 3 × 5 = 15 cups: 6 : 15, equivalent to 2 : 5.

Watch out: adding the same amount to both is not an equivalent ratio. 2 : 5 plus 2 each is 4 : 7, weaker lemonade. Two batches is 4 : 10.

Part C · Lessons 6–9

Double number lines

Learn chapter →

Know it

  • A double number line shows equivalent ratios: one amount on the top line, the other on the bottom, and each tick pairs amounts in the same ratio.
  • Ticks are evenly spaced, and both lines start at 0.
  • How much for one? Split both lines into the same number of equal parts. The price for one is the unit price.
  • At a constant speed, something goes the same distance every minute (or second, or hour). Find the distance in 1 minute, then multiply.

Example

5 pounds of apples cost $15. How much do 2 pounds cost?

1 pound: $15 ÷ 5 = $3. 2 pounds: 2 × $3 = $6.

Watch out: 3 fewer pounds doesn’t mean $3 less ($12 is wrong). Find the amount for 1 first, then multiply.

Part D · Lessons 8–10

Better deals

Learn chapter →

Know it

  • To compare two prices, find the price for one in each. Less for each is the better deal, even when the total is more.
  • To compare two speeds, find the distance each goes in one second (or minute, or hour). More for each is faster.
  • Another way: make one amount the same in both (the same number of cups of white paint), then compare the other.

Example

Which is the better deal: 3 bags of chips for $6, or 5 bags for $8?

OfferPrice for 1 bag
3 for $6$6 ÷ 3 = $2.00
5 for $8$8 ÷ 5 = $1.60

5 bags for $8 is the better deal: it costs more in all, but less for each bag.

Watch out: the smaller total isn’t always the better deal, and the longer distance isn’t always the faster runner. Compare the amounts for one.

Part E · Lessons 11–14

Tables

Learn chapter →

Know it

  • A table of equivalent ratios has one pair in each row. Every row is another row times the same number, on both sides.
  • Rows can go in any order, and they don’t have to go up by the same amount.
  • Go through 1 when the numbers don’t multiply evenly: divide to find the amount for one, then multiply. 4 notebooks cost $10, so 1 costs $10 ÷ 4 = $2.50, and 6 cost 6 × $2.50 = $15.
  • A table and a double number line show the same pairs.

Example

Juice is mixed 3 cups of juice to 5 cups of water. How much water goes with 12 cups of juice?

3 × 4 = 12, so the water is 5 × 4 = 20 cups.

Watch out: don’t add across. 12 cups of juice is 9 more than 3, but 5 + 9 = 14 cups of water is wrong. Multiply both columns by the same number.

Part F · Lessons 15–16

Part-part-whole

Learn chapter →

Know it

  • A tape diagram shows a ratio as equal boxes: 2 : 3 is 2 boxes and 3 boxes, 5 boxes in all.
  • From the total: split it evenly into all the boxes. one box = total ÷ (a + b).
  • From one part: divide it by that part’s boxes to find one box. Then multiply to find the other part, or the total.

Example

A shelter has dogs and cats in the ratio 2 : 3, 25 animals in all. How many are cats?

2 + 3 = 5 boxes, so each box is 25 ÷ 5 = 5. Cats: 3 × 5 = 15. (Dogs: 10.)

Watch out: divide the total by all the boxes (5), not by one part’s boxes. 25 ÷ 3 doesn’t give a box.
