Part A · Lessons 2–4

Converting units

Learn chapter →

Know it

  • The same length (or weight, or amount) takes more of a small unit and fewer of a big one.
  • Bigger units to smaller: multiply by how many small units are in one big one. 4 yd = 4 × 3 = 12 ft.
  • Smaller units to bigger: divide. 30 in = 30 ÷ 12 = 2.5 ft.
  • Pick the unit that makes the number easy to picture: a pencil is 18 cm, not 0.00018 km.

Example

A rope is 4 yards long. How many feet is that?

Each yard is 3 feet: 4 × 3 = 12 feet.

Watch out: 4 ÷ 3 ≈ 1.33 feet is wrong. A foot is smaller than a yard, so it takes more feet: multiply.

Part B · Lessons 1, 6–7

Unit rates

Learn chapter →

Know it

  • A unit rate is how much of one thing goes with 1 of the other. “Per” means “for each.”
  • Every ratio has two unit rates. 12 muffins from 3 cups of flour is 12 ÷ 3 = 4 muffins per cup, and 3 ÷ 12 = 0.25 cup per muffin.
  • Use the one that fits the question: “how many A for each B” is A ÷ B.
  • Equivalent ratios have the same unit rates.

Example

A printer prints 30 pages in 5 minutes. How long does it take to print 90 pages?

30 ÷ 5 = 6 pages per minute, so 90 pages take 90 ÷ 6 = 15 minutes.

Watch out: muffins per cup is 12 ÷ 3, not 3 ÷ 12 (that’s cups per muffin). Check that the answer makes sense: 1 cup makes more than 1 muffin.

Part C · Lessons 5, 8–9

Speed and pace

Learn chapter →

Know it

  • Speed is distance for each unit of time, like miles per hour. A bigger speed is faster.
  • Pace is time for each unit of distance, like minutes per mile. A smaller pace is faster.
  • Time for a trip: distance × pace, or distance ÷ speed.
  • Distance in a time: speed × time.

Example

Diego bikes 12 miles in 48 minutes at a constant speed. How long do 20 miles take?

Pace: 48 ÷ 12 = 4 minutes per mile. 20 miles: 20 × 4 = 80 minutes.

Watch out: a bigger pace is slower. 10 minutes per mile takes longer than 8 minutes per mile.

Part D · Lessons 10–12

Percentages

Learn chapter →

Know it

  • Percent means “for each 100.” A dollar is 100 cents, so 25¢ is 25% of a dollar.
  • 100% is the whole. Split the whole into 10 equal parts: each is 10%.
  • On a double number line, the amounts go on one line and the percents on the other, with the whole at 100%.
  • Percents can go past 100%: 150% is one and a half times the whole.

Example

A tank holds 80 liters. How many liters is 30% of the tank?

10%: 80 ÷ 10 = 8 liters. 30%: 3 × 8 = 24 liters.

Watch out: 30% of 80 isn’t 80 − 30 = 50, and it isn’t 80 × 30. It’s 30 hundredths of 80: 0.3 × 80 = 24.

Part E · Lesson 13

Benchmark percents

Learn chapter →

Know it

  • Some percents are easy fractions, so you can find them in your head:
percentfractionto find it
10%1/10÷ 10
25%1/4÷ 4
50%1/2÷ 2
75%3/4÷ 4, then × 3
  • A discount is the amount taken off. Sale price = price − discount. Or: 25% off leaves 75% of the price.

Example

Shoes cost $60. They are 25% off. What is the sale price?

Discount: 25% is 1/4, so $60 ÷ 4 = $15.

Sale price: $60 − $15 = $45.

Or: 75% of $60 is $15 × 3 = $45.

Watch out: $15 is the discount, not the sale price. And $60 − 25 = $35 takes off 25 dollars, not 25 percent.

Part F · Lessons 14–16

Part, whole, or percent

Learn chapter →

Know it

  • A percent problem has three numbers: the part, the whole, and the percent. Know two, find the third.
  • Find the part: take the percent of the whole.
  • Find the whole: work backward through 10%. If 12 is 30%, then each tenth is 12 ÷ 3 = 4, and the whole is 10 × 4 = 40.
  • Find the percent: part ÷ whole, as hundredths. 18 out of 24 is 18 ÷ 24 = 0.75: 75%.

Example

A tank has 15 liters in it, and it’s 60% full. How much does it hold?

60% is 6 tenths: 15 ÷ 6 = 2.5 liters in each. Whole: 10 × 2.5 = 25 liters.

Watch out: “15 is 60% of what?” isn’t 60% of 15 (that’s 9). And divide the part by the whole: 24 ÷ 18 gives more than 100%.
