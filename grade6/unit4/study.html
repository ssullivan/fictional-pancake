Part A · Lessons 1–3

Making sense of division

Learn chapter →

Know it

  • In 12 ÷ 4 = 3, 12 is the dividend, 4 is the divisor, and 3 is the quotient.
  • Estimate before you divide: a divisor bigger than the dividend gives less than 1; a divisor less than 1 gives more than the dividend: 6 ÷ 1⁄2 = 12.
  • Division answers two questions. 24 bagels in 3 boxes: how many in each box? 24 bagels, 3 to a box: how many boxes? Both are 24 ÷ 3 = 8.
  • Every equal-groups problem has groups × size = total. Multiply when the total is unknown; divide when either of the others is.

Example

One equation, three questions:

QuestionEquation
3 bags, 4 apples in each. How many apples?3 × 4 = ?
12 apples, 4 in a bag. How many bags?? × 4 = 12
12 apples in 3 bags. How many in each?3 × ? = 12

The last two are both 12 ÷ something: 12 ÷ 4 = 3 bags, 12 ÷ 3 = 4 apples.

Watch out: dividing doesn’t always make a number smaller. 6 ÷ 1⁄2 asks how many halves are in 6: 12.

Part B · Lessons 4–6

How many groups?

Learn chapter →

Know it

  • “How many 3⁄4s are in 3?” is 3 ÷ 3⁄4.
  • Draw the dividend as a tape. Cut it into the divisor’s pieces (fourths), then group them: 3 is 12 fourths, which make 4 groups of 3 fourths.
  • When pieces are left over, count them as a fraction of a group: 2 cups hold 8 fourths, 2 groups of 3 fourths and 2 fourths more, which is 2⁄3 of a group. 2 ÷ 3⁄4 = 2 2⁄3.
  • Hops on a ruler work the same way: how many hops of 3⁄8 inch from 0 to 3?

Example

A bag holds 3 pounds of rice, and a family eats 3⁄4 pound a week. How many weeks does it last?

12 fourths make 4 groups of 3: 3 ÷ 3⁄4 = 4 weeks. Check: 4 × 3⁄4 = 3.

Watch out: 3 × 3⁄4 = 2 1⁄4 answers a different question. And a leftover is a fraction of a group: 2 fourths left out of a group of 3 fourths is 2⁄3, not 2⁄4.

Part C · Lesson 7

What fraction of a group?

Learn chapter →

Know it

  • When the dividend is less than the divisor, there isn’t even one group: the quotient is less than 1.
  • It tells what fraction of a group you have: 1⁄2 ÷ 3⁄4 asks what fraction of 3⁄4 cup is 1⁄2 cup.
  • “How many times as long (or as much)?” compares two amounts the same way: first ÷ second.

Example

A batch of pancakes needs 3⁄4 cup of milk. Elena has 1⁄2 cup. What fraction of a batch can Elena make?

1⁄2 cup is 2 fourths, and a batch is 3 fourths: 1⁄2 ÷ 3⁄4 = 2⁄3 of a batch.

Watch out: 3⁄4 ÷ 1⁄2 = 1 1⁄2 is the other way around. Less milk than a batch can’t make more than 1 batch.

Part D · Lessons 8–9

How much in each group?

Learn chapter →

Know it

  • Sometimes you know how much fills part of a group, and need the whole group.
  • Divide by the fraction: if 1 1⁄2 cups fill 3⁄4 of a pitcher, the pitcher holds 1 1⁄2 ÷ 3⁄4 cups.
  • Think it through with the tape: 3 fourths hold 1 1⁄2 cups, so each fourth holds 1⁄2 cup, and 4 fourths hold 2 cups.
  • “How much for 1?” works the same way: 3 1⁄2 yards cost $21, so 1 yard costs 21 ÷ 3 1⁄2 = $6.

Example

1 1⁄2 cups of water fill 3⁄4 of a pitcher. How much does the whole pitcher hold?

3⁄2 ÷ 3⁄4 = 3⁄2 × 4⁄3 = 2 cups.

Watch out: 3⁄4 of 1 1⁄2 cups is the wrong question: 1 1⁄2 cups is the part, so the whole is more.

Part E · Lessons 10–11

Dividing fractions

Learn chapter →

Know it

  • Dividing by a whole number is multiplying by a unit fraction: 15 ÷ 5 = 15 × 1⁄5 = 3.
  • Dividing by 1⁄4 counts fourths: multiply by 4. 4 1⁄2 ÷ 1⁄4 = 18.
  • Dividing by 3⁄4: multiply by 4 (count fourths), then divide by 3 (group them). That’s multiplying by the reciprocal: ÷ 3⁄4 is × 4⁄3.
  • With the same denominator, divide the tops: 5⁄6 ÷ 2⁄6 = 5 ÷ 2 = 2 1⁄2.
  • Estimate first, so you can tell if an answer makes sense.

Example

Find 15⁄4 ÷ 5⁄8.

Estimate: about 4 ÷ 1⁄2, so about 8.

Flip the divisor and multiply: 15⁄4 × 8⁄5 = 120⁄20 = 6.

Check: 6 × 5⁄8 = 30⁄8 = 15⁄4.

Watch out: flip only the divisor (the second number). Flipping both, or multiplying without flipping, gives a different answer.

Part F · Lessons 12–13

Lengths and areas

Learn chapter →

Know it

  • “How many times as long?” divides one length by the other.
  • The area of a rectangle with fractional sides is still length × width. Write mixed numbers as fractions first.
  • On a grid, the rectangle covers whole squares and parts of squares that add up to the same area.
  • If you know the area and one side, divide to find the other side.

Example

Find the area of a rectangle 2 1⁄2 units by 1 1⁄2 units.

5⁄2 × 3⁄2 = 15⁄4 = 3 3⁄4 square units.

On the grid: 2 whole squares, 3 halves, and a quarter: 2 + 1 1⁄2 + 1⁄4.

Watch out: 2 × 1 = 2 multiplies only the whole numbers and leaves out the halves. Write each side as a fraction.

Part G · Lessons 14–15

Triangles and prisms

Learn chapter →

Know it

  • A triangle’s area is 1⁄2 × base × height, with fractions too.
  • To find a missing height: height = 2 × area ÷ base.
  • A box’s volume is length × width × height, with fractional edges too.
  • Small cubes fill a box in three directions: cubes with 1⁄2-inch edges fit 2 along each inch, so 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 fill each cubic inch.

Example

A box is 3 feet long, 1 1⁄2 feet wide, and 2⁄3 foot tall. What is its volume?

3 × 3⁄2 = 9⁄2 square feet of floor.

9⁄2 × 2⁄3 = 18⁄6 = 3 cubic feet.

How many cubes with 1⁄2-foot edges fill a 2-foot cube? 4 along each edge: 4 × 4 × 4 = 64.

Watch out: small cubes fill all three directions. A 1-inch cube holds 8 half-inch cubes, not 2.

Part H · Lesson 16

Putting it together

Learn chapter →

Know it

  • Write the equation with a ? first: groups × size = total. Is the total unknown (multiply), or the groups or the size (divide)?
  • A fraction of an amount is multiplying: 2⁄3 of 6 miles is 2⁄3 × 6 = 4.
  • When a recipe uses two things, find the batches each one allows. The smaller number is how many you can make.

Example

A recipe uses 3⁄4 cup of flour and 1⁄3 cup of butter. Han has 3 cups of flour and 1 cup of butter. How many whole batches can Han make?

Flour: 3 ÷ 3⁄4 = 4 batches.

Butter: 1 ÷ 1⁄3 = 3 batches.

Each batch needs both, so Han can make 3 batches.

Watch out: don’t add the batches (4 + 3 = 7). Each batch needs flour and butter, so the smaller number wins.
